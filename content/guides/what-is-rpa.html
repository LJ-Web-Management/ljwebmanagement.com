RPA, or robotic process automation, is software that uses "bots" to operate applications the way a person does: clicking buttons, typing into fields, copying data between screens, and downloading files. The bot follows a script of exact steps, so it can automate repetitive work in systems that offer no other way to connect.

RPA is widely used by large companies with older systems. For small businesses, it is a useful tool in specific situations, and the wrong tool in many others. This guide explains the difference.

How RPA works

An RPA bot is recorded or configured to repeat a sequence of actions on screen. For example:

  1. Open the insurance portal and log in.
  2. Search for the patient's policy number.
  3. Copy the eligibility status and copay into a spreadsheet.
  4. Repeat for every appointment tomorrow.

The bot identifies buttons and fields by their position, label, or the page structure behind them. It can run on a schedule, on demand, or when triggered by another system. Some bots run "attended," alongside a person at their desk; others run "unattended" on a server.

RPA use cases

  • Legacy software: entering data into older desktop applications that have no API.
  • Portals: checking insurance eligibility, filing on government or carrier portals, downloading statements from supplier websites.
  • Report downloads: logging in to systems each morning to export reports that cannot be scheduled.
  • Data migration: moving records from an old system to a new one when no import tool exists.
  • Reconciliation: comparing figures across systems that cannot talk to each other.

The rule of thumb: if an app has an API or integration, use it. Reach for RPA only for the systems that leave you no other option.

RPA and AI together

Classic RPA cannot interpret anything; it only follows steps. Pairing it with AI expands what it can do. An AI step can read an emailed document and extract the fields, then a bot enters them into a legacy system. This combination is sometimes called intelligent automation. Is RPA right for your business?

Consider RPA when all of these are true:

  • The task is high volume and fully rule-based
  • The system involved has no API, integration, or reliable export
  • Replacing the system is not realistic in the near term
  • Someone can maintain the bot when screens change

If the system can be replaced with a modern, connected tool, that is often the better long-term investment. Everything else in your stack can usually be automated more simply with workflow automation.

Getting started

  1. List the systems your team copies data into or out of by hand.
  2. Check each one for an API, integration, or scheduled export. Many have more than people realize.
  3. For the ones that truly have none, pilot a single bot on a high-volume task and measure its reliability for a month.
  4. Document the bot's steps and assign an owner who checks its runs.

Automation should earn its place. This final lesson shows how to prove an automation is working, put a number on what it returns, and use that evidence to decide what to automate next.

Measure before you launch

Pick one or two numbers the automation should move, and record them for a few weeks before launch:

AutomationBaseline to record
Lead follow-upAverage time to first reply, share of leads followed up, close rate
Appointment remindersNo-show rate, staff time spent on reminder calls
Invoicing and remindersDays from job completion to invoice, average days to get paid
Data entryHours per week spent on entry, error rate
ReportingHours to build the weekly report, how often it is late

If you skip this, you will be left with "it feels faster," which is hard to build on.

Count both kinds of value

  • Time saved: hours removed per month multiplied by the fully loaded hourly cost of whoever did the work. The time savings calculator does this.
  • Revenue protected or gained: more leads converted, fewer missed appointments, faster payments, fewer errors. These are often worth more than the hours. Try the lead response, no-show, and late payment calculators.

Payback and ROI

Two simple formulas cover most decisions:

  • Monthly net benefit = value of time saved + revenue gained − running costs.
  • Payback period = build cost ÷ monthly net benefit.

An automation that costs $2,000 to build and nets $500 a month pays for itself in four months. Everything after that is return. For a fuller view over several years, use the automation payback calculator or read the automation ROI guide.

Be conservative

Use cautious estimates, and run the numbers with revenue gains at zero first. If the automation pays back on time savings alone, the revenue upside is a bonus. If it only works with optimistic revenue numbers, look for a simpler version.

Review after 30 and 90 days

Compare your numbers against the baseline and ask:

  • Did the target number move as expected?
  • How many errors or exceptions needed a person, and why?
  • Is the team actually using the time it got back?
  • What did customers notice, good or bad?
  • What small change would make it better?

Build your automation roadmap

With one success behind you, return to your scored task list from lesson two. Re-score it with what you have learned, then work down the list one workflow at a time. Look for reuse: the CRM cleanup you did for lead follow-up makes onboarding easier; the invoice trigger you built makes review requests trivial.

Over time, the individual workflows join up into a system: inquiries become customers, jobs become invoices, invoices become payments and reviews, with people focused on the work only they can do.

You now know how language models work, how to prompt them, where they fit in a workflow, and how to keep them safe. This final lesson puts it together into a plan you can use for your first AI automation project, whether you build it yourself or with a partner.

Step 1: Choose the right first task

A good first AI project is:

  • Frequent: it happens daily, so the time savings are real and you get lots of examples to learn from
  • Language-heavy: it involves reading, sorting, summarizing, extracting, or writing
  • Easy to judge: a person can quickly tell a good result from a bad one
  • Low risk: a mistake can be caught before it matters

Strong candidates: sorting a shared inbox, drafting replies to routine questions, summarizing calls into CRM notes, extracting data from invoices or forms, and turning meeting notes into action items. Use the priority scorer to compare your options.

Step 2: Define success

Before building, write down:

  • The baseline: how long the task takes today, how many per week, and the current error rate or delay.
  • The quality bar: what a good output looks like, ideally with a few real examples.
  • The target: for example, "cut time spent on inbox triage from six hours a week to one, with fewer than 5% of emails misrouted."

Step 3: Design the workflow

Sketch it using the pattern from lesson three:

  1. Trigger: what starts it (a new email, a new file, a finished call)
  2. Prepare: gather and trim the input; retrieve any reference information the AI needs
  3. AI step: your tested prompt, with a fixed output format
  4. Validate: rule-based checks on the output
  5. Review: a person approves, edits, or rejects (at least during the pilot)
  6. Act: update the CRM, send the reply, file the document
  7. Log: keep the input, output, and any edits

Estimate the running cost with the AI cost calculator. For most first projects it is small next to the time saved.

Step 4: Pilot with real examples

  • Test the prompt on 20 or more real past examples before going live, and fix failure patterns.
  • Run the pilot for two to four weeks with human review on every output.
  • Track the edit rate: how often the reviewer had to meaningfully change or reject the output, by category.
  • Collect the team's feedback weekly. They will spot problems and improvements faster than any dashboard.

Step 5: Expand carefully

When the edit rate for a category is consistently low, consider letting that category run without approval, while still reviewing a random sample each week. Keep human approval for sensitive categories indefinitely. Then compare against your baseline, write up the result, and pick the next project.

A realistic expectation

The first AI automation usually takes longer to get right than people expect, mostly in testing and refining the prompt. The second one goes much faster, because the patterns, review habits, and integrations are already in place.

# Build vs Buy: When Custom Software, Apps, or a SaaS Product Make Sense

> A decision framework for buying off-the-shelf software, configuring and integrating it, or building custom software, mobile apps, marketplaces, and SaaS…

A decision framework for buying off-the-shelf software, configuring and integrating it, or building custom software, mobile apps, marketplaces, and SaaS products, plus how to keep custom software maintained.

LJ Web ManagementAutomation team Updated October 25, 2026 4 min read

Key takeaways

- Buy for commodity functions like accounting and payroll; build only where your process is a real competitive difference.
- There is a middle path: buy a platform, then configure, integrate, and automate it around your workflow.
- Total cost of ownership includes hosting, security, support, and ongoing development, not just the initial build.
- Own your code, data, and infrastructure accounts, and plan maintenance before you launch.

Every growing business eventually hits a moment where the software it can buy does not quite fit. The team builds workarounds, spreadsheets multiply, and someone asks: "Should we just build our own?" Sometimes the answer is yes. Often there is a better option in between. This guide gives you a framework for deciding.

## Three options, not two

| Option | What it means | Best when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buy | Use off-the-shelf software as designed | The function is a commodity: accounting, payroll, email, basic CRM |
| Buy and extend | Configure a platform, then integrate and automate it around your workflow | A good platform exists but needs to connect to your other systems |
| Build | Develop custom software you own | Your process is a competitive advantage, or you plan to sell the software |

The middle option is the most common winner. Integrations through [Zapier, Make, or n8n](https://www.ljwebmanagement.com/guides/zapier-vs-make-vs-n8n/), or [custom APIs](https://www.ljwebmanagement.com/solutions/erp-enterprise-systems/systems-integration-api/), let off-the-shelf tools behave like one system without building everything from scratch.

## A five-question decision framework

1. **Is this process a competitive difference?** If customers choose you because of how you do it, building may protect that advantage. If not, buy.
2. **Does a platform cover at least 80 percent of the need?** If yes, buy and extend.
3. **How many systems must it combine?** When the job spans many tools and users juggle several logins, a custom layer such as a [portal](https://www.ljwebmanagement.com/solutions/customer-experience/customer-portal/) or internal app can unify them.
4. **What does licensing cost at your future scale?** Per-seat pricing for a large team using a small slice of features can justify building.
5. **Could others pay for it?** If your internal tool solves a common industry problem, it may become a [SaaS product](https://www.ljwebmanagement.com/solutions/industry-platforms-saas/custom-saas-development/).

## Common custom builds and when they fit

### Customer and staff apps

[Mobile apps](https://www.ljwebmanagement.com/solutions/industry-platforms-saas/mobile-app-development/) make sense when customers interact often (ordering, booking, loyalty) or when field staff need offline data capture, scanning, and photos. Otherwise a fast, mobile-friendly website usually serves customers just as well.

### Marketplaces

Connecting many vendors with many buyers needs onboarding, routing, commissions, and split payouts. Platforms like Sharetribe can launch quickly; a custom [marketplace](https://www.ljwebmanagement.com/solutions/industry-platforms-saas/marketplace-platform/) fits when workflows or scale demand it.

### Industry platforms

Restaurants, property managers, and other specialized operators often start with an industry platform such as a [restaurant POS](https://www.ljwebmanagement.com/solutions/industry-platforms-saas/restaurant-pos/) or [property management platform](https://www.ljwebmanagement.com/solutions/industry-platforms-saas/property-management-platform/), then extend it with integrations and automations.

## Total cost of ownership

Compare options over three to five years, not just the first invoice:

- Licenses or build cost.
- Implementation, data migration, and training.
- Integrations and their maintenance.
- Hosting, monitoring, backups, and security updates.
- Support and ongoing feature development.
- The cost of manual workarounds you eliminate (or keep).

**Software is never finished**

Operating systems update, browsers change, security vulnerabilities are discovered, and your business evolves. Plan and budget for ongoing care before you build, through an in-house team or a [managed SaaS service](https://www.ljwebmanagement.com/solutions/industry-platforms-saas/managed-saas/).

## How to de-risk a custom build

1. **Define the smallest useful version** that solves the core problem for one group of users.
2. **Prototype first:** clickable designs tested with real users before code is written.
3. **Build in short cycles** with working software reviewed every week or two.
4. **Own everything:** source code repository, cloud accounts, domains, and data in your name.
5. **Document and monitor** so any qualified developer can maintain it.

## Red flags when hiring a development partner

- They will not put code ownership in writing.
- Hosting must stay in their accounts.
- No plan for testing, security, or maintenance.
- A fixed scope with no discovery or prototyping phase for a complex product.

## Frequently asked questions

**When should a business build custom software?**

When your process is a genuine competitive advantage that off-the-shelf tools cannot support, when you need to combine several systems into one experience, when per-seat licensing has become very expensive, or when you plan to sell the software to others.

**Is custom software more expensive than buying?**

Upfront, usually yes. Over several years it can be comparable or cheaper if it replaces several subscriptions or eliminates significant manual work. Compare total cost of ownership, including maintenance, over three to five years.

**Who owns custom software?**

That depends on your contract. You should insist on owning the source code, data, and the cloud accounts it runs in, so you are never locked in to one developer.

**What happens after custom software launches?**

Software needs ongoing hosting, monitoring, security updates, bug fixes, and improvements. Plan for this with an in-house team or a managed service before launch.

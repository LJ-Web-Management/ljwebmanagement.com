# How to Calculate the ROI of Business Automation

> A simple, honest method for estimating what an automation will save and earn, including the costs people forget, with worked examples and a payback…

A simple, honest method for estimating what an automation will save and earn, including the costs people forget, with worked examples and a payback calculation.

LJ Web ManagementAutomation team Updated October 25, 2026 4 min read

Key takeaways

- ROI comes from three places: time saved, revenue recovered, and errors avoided.
- Use loaded labor cost (wage plus taxes and benefits), not just the hourly wage.
- Include every cost: build, software subscriptions, and ongoing maintenance.
- Payback period is often the most useful number for a small business decision.

"Will it pay for itself?" is the right question to ask about any automation. Unfortunately, the answers you find online are often either vague ("save hundreds of hours!") or so detailed that nobody fills them in. This guide gives you a method that takes about 20 minutes and produces a number you can trust enough to make a decision.

## The three sources of return

1. **Time saved.** Hours your team no longer spends on the task, valued at what those hours cost you.
2. **Revenue recovered or gained.** Leads that no longer go cold, appointments that no longer go empty, invoices paid sooner, renewals that no longer lapse.
3. **Errors and risk avoided.** Re-work from data entry mistakes, missed deadlines, compliance penalties, and refunds.

Most people only count the first one. For customer-facing automations, the second is often larger.

## Step 1: Calculate time saved

**Monthly hours saved = runs per month × minutes per run ÷ 60 × share automated**

"Share automated" accounts for exceptions. If a person still handles about 10 percent of cases, use 0.9.

Then value it: **Monthly labor savings = hours saved × loaded hourly cost**. Loaded cost includes payroll taxes, benefits, and overhead, and is commonly 1.25 to 1.4 times the base wage for small businesses. Use your own numbers if you have them.

**Time saved is capacity, not always cash**

Unless you reduce hours or avoid a hire, saved time shows up as capacity: the same team handles more customers or spends time on higher-value work. That is real value, but be honest with yourself about how it will be used.

## Step 2: Estimate revenue impact conservatively

Pick the one metric the automation moves and estimate a modest improvement:

| Automation | Metric it moves | Revenue formula |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lead follow-up | Lead-to-customer rate | Extra customers per month × average job value × margin |
| Appointment reminders | No-show rate | Recovered appointments per month × average ticket |
| Invoicing and reminders | Days to payment, write-offs | Write-offs avoided, plus the value of cash arriving sooner |
| Renewal reminders | Renewal rate | Extra renewals per month × renewal value |

Use your own historical numbers wherever possible, and pick an improvement you would be comfortable defending. You can always revise upward once you have data.

## Step 3: Add up the full cost

- The build (a flat quote or internal time to design, build, and test)
- Automation platform subscription (Zapier, Make, n8n, or similar)
- New tool subscriptions, if any are needed
- Usage fees, such as SMS messages or AI model calls
- Ongoing maintenance and support

## Step 4: Calculate ROI and payback

**Monthly net benefit = labor savings + revenue impact + errors avoided − monthly running costs**

**Payback period (months) = build cost ÷ monthly net benefit**

**First-year ROI = (12 × monthly net benefit − build cost) ÷ build cost**

For small businesses, payback period is often the most intuitive number. "This pays for itself in three months" is easier to act on than a percentage.

## A worked example

Here is an illustrative example with made-up but realistic numbers for a home services company automating lead follow-up and missed-call text-back:

- About 160 inquiries a month; the office spends roughly 6 minutes per inquiry on first replies and follow-ups. With 85 percent handled automatically: 160 × 6 ÷ 60 × 0.85 ≈ **13.6 hours a month**.
- Loaded cost of $30 an hour: 13.6 × $30 ≈ **$408 a month** in labor.
- Faster response converts 3 extra jobs a month at a $900 average with 40 percent margin: 3 × $900 × 0.4 = **$1,080 a month**.
- Running costs (platform plus texting) of about **$90 a month**.

Monthly net benefit ≈ $408 + $1,080 − $90 = **$1,398**. With a $3,000 build, payback ≈ 3,000 ÷ 1,398 ≈ **2.1 months**, and first-year ROI ≈ (12 × 1,398 − 3,000) ÷ 3,000 ≈ **459 percent**.

Your numbers will differ, which is the point: plug in your own. The [ROI calculator](https://www.ljwebmanagement.com/roi-calculator/) does the labor side in a few seconds.

## Prove it after launch

Write down the baseline before you automate (response time, no-show rate, days to payment, hours spent), then check the same numbers after 30 and 90 days. A simple automated weekly report on those metrics makes the result visible to everyone and keeps the automation from being forgotten.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a good ROI for automation?**

It depends on your alternatives, but many well-chosen small business automations pay for themselves within a few months because they remove frequent, repetitive work. If a project's estimated payback is longer than a year, look for a simpler version or a different first project.

**How do I measure time saved by automation?**

Count how often the task happens and time a few typical runs before you automate, including switching between tools. After launch, track how often a person still has to step in. The difference, multiplied by loaded hourly cost, is your labor savings.

**Should I count revenue gains or just cost savings?**

Count both, but separately. Cost savings are usually easier to prove. Revenue gains (more leads converted, fewer no-shows, faster payments) are often larger, so estimate them conservatively and confirm them with before-and-after data.

**What costs do people forget when estimating automation ROI?**

Software subscriptions for the automation platform and any new tools, messaging fees for SMS, maintenance when connected apps change, and the internal time spent defining the process and testing. A flat-fee build helps keep the main cost predictable.

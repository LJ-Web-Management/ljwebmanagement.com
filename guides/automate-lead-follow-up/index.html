    How to Automate Lead Follow-Up: A Speed-to-Lead Playbook

    Respond to every new lead in minutes, follow up until they answer, and hand hot leads to a person at the right moment. A practical playbook with message templates.

    LJ Web ManagementAutomation team Updated October 25, 2026 6 min read

    Key takeaways

    • Response time is the biggest lever: the first business to reply usually gets the conversation.
    • Automate the first touch within a minute, by text and email, on every channel leads come from.
    • Follow up on a schedule (often 6 to 8 touches over two weeks) and stop the moment the lead replies.
    • Route hot leads to a person immediately and log everything in the CRM automatically.

    Most small businesses do not have a lead generation problem. They have a lead response problem. The inquiry comes in while the owner is on a job site, the office manager is on another call, or everyone has gone home for the day. By the time someone replies, the prospect has already talked to a competitor who answered first.

    Lead follow-up is usually the highest-return automation a service business can build, because it sits directly in front of revenue and follows clear rules. This playbook covers how to design it.

    Why speed matters so much

    People who fill out a form or call a business are usually comparing options right then. A widely cited Harvard Business Review study of online leads found that firms which contacted prospects within an hour were dramatically more likely to qualify them than firms that waited even a little longer, and that many companies took more than a day to respond at all.

    The practical takeaway is simple: whoever replies first and helpfully usually gets the conversation. Automation lets you reply first every time, including nights and weekends, without hiring someone to watch the inbox.

    The anatomy of an automated follow-up system

    A complete lead follow-up automation has five parts. Skipping any of them is where most do-it-yourself setups break down.

    1. Capture. Every lead source (website forms, landing pages, Facebook and Google lead ads, marketplace inquiries, phone calls, chat) feeds into one place, usually your CRM.
    2. Instant first touch. Within a minute, the lead gets a short text and an email confirming you received their request, with a next step such as a booking link.
    3. Internal alert. The right person on your team is notified by text, Slack, or a CRM task, with the lead's details attached.
    4. Follow-up cadence. If the lead has not replied or booked, a sequence of messages continues on a set schedule.
    5. Stop and hand off. The moment the lead replies, books, or is marked as won or lost, the automation stops and a person takes over.
    The rule most setups miss

    The sequence must stop automatically when the lead responds. Nothing erodes trust faster than a "just checking in!" text an hour after someone already booked.

    Step 1: Put every lead source in one pipeline

    List every place leads come from today. For most businesses it is a mix of website forms, phone calls, ad platforms, email, and directories. Then connect each one to your CRM so a new record is created automatically, tagged with its source. If you do not have a CRM yet, a well-structured Airtable base or Google Sheet can work as a starting point.

    Two details matter here: deduplication (a person who fills out two forms should not get two sequences) and source tracking (you will want to know which channels produce booked jobs, not just leads).

    Step 2: Write a first reply that earns a response

    The first message has one job: confirm you are real and responsive, and make the next step easy. Keep it under 300 characters for text. For example:

    Hi Jordan, this is Maria with Northside Plumbing. Thanks for reaching out about your water heater. I can get you on the schedule this week. What's the best time for a quick call, or you can pick a slot here: [booking link]

    Notice what it does: uses their name and their request, names a real person, offers two easy next steps, and does not dump a wall of information. The matching email can include more detail, such as service areas, reviews, or what to expect.

    Step 3: Build the follow-up cadence

    A reasonable starting cadence for service businesses looks like this. Adjust the timing to your sales cycle.

    WhenChannelPurpose
    Within 1 minuteText and emailAcknowledge, offer a booking link
    Within 5 minutesCall task for your teamA person tries to call while interest is highest
    Same day, eveningTextShort nudge if no reply
    Day 2EmailHelpful detail: pricing ranges, FAQs, or reviews
    Day 4TextSpecific question that is easy to answer
    Day 7EmailSocial proof or a relevant example
    Day 14TextPolite close-the-loop message

    The "close the loop" message often gets the most replies, because it gives people permission to say "not right now" or "actually, yes." Something like: "Hi Jordan, I don't want to keep bugging you. Should I close out your request, or would you still like help with the water heater?"

    Step 4: Route and alert the right person

    Automation should make your team faster, not replace the human conversation. Set up rules that decide who gets each lead:

    • By territory or service: commercial vs. residential, or north vs. south service area.
    • Round robin: rotate leads evenly across sales reps or estimators.
    • By value: large or urgent requests go straight to an owner or senior rep.

    Alerts should include everything needed to act in one glance: name, phone, what they asked for, source, and a one-tap link to the CRM record.

    Step 5: Recover the leads that went quiet

    Two more automations close the remaining gaps. A quote or estimate follow-up sequence runs after you send pricing and stops when the quote is accepted or declined. A stalled-deal alert flags any lead sitting in the same pipeline stage for too long, so a manager can step in before it goes cold for good.

    Where AI fits in

    Rule-based automation handles most of this playbook. AI adds value in three specific places:

    • Reading free-text inquiries to classify the request type and urgency before routing.
    • Drafting personalized replies that a person reviews before sending, for complex or high-value requests.
    • Summarizing call notes into the CRM so the next touch has context.

    Keep a human in the loop for anything that commits you to a price, a date, or a promise.

    Measure what matters

    Track these numbers before and after launch, ideally by lead source:

    • Median time to first response
    • Percentage of leads that receive a reply from a person within one business day
    • Lead-to-appointment (or lead-to-quote) rate
    • Close rate and revenue by source

    Frequently asked questions

    How fast should you respond to a new lead?

    As fast as you can, ideally within five minutes and certainly within the hour. Research published in Harvard Business Review found that companies contacting leads within an hour were far more likely to qualify them than companies that waited longer. An automated first reply makes a sub-minute response realistic even after hours.

    How many times should you follow up with a lead?

    Most leads need more than one or two touches. A common pattern is six to eight touches over about two weeks across text, email, and phone, front-loaded in the first 48 hours. The sequence should stop automatically as soon as the lead replies or books.

    Is it legal to text leads automatically?

    In the United States, automated texting is governed by the TCPA and carrier rules, so you need the person's consent, a clear identification of your business, and a working opt-out (such as replying STOP). Business texting through local numbers also requires 10DLC registration through your provider. Rules vary by situation, so confirm your consent language with counsel.

    Will automated follow-up feel robotic?

    Not if you write it like a person and keep it short. Use the lead's name and the service they asked about, sign messages from a real team member, and hand the conversation to a human the moment the lead replies.

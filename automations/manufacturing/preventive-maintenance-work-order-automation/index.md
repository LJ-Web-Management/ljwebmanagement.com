How it works

## What this automation does, step by step

1. Running-hour data pulled from equipment PLCs (or logged manually by operators when a machine isn't networked) is checked daily against each asset's service interval, and once a machine crosses 90% of its due threshold this automation generates a maintenance work order in the CMMS, assigns it to the appropriate technician, and checks parts inventory to see whether the commonly needed filters, belts, or bearings are on the shelf.
2. If a part is below the reorder point, a purchase order draft is created automatically so the part arrives before the service date rather than after the machine has already gone down.
3. The maintenance manager receives a weekly view of every upcoming and overdue work order sorted by criticality, turning what used to be a wall calendar and a gut feeling into a system that catches the 30-day service window before it becomes an unplanned Tuesday-afternoon breakdown.

## The manual way vs. the automated way

### Without automation

The manual version of this puts the entire workload on a maintenance manager, who has to juggle phone calls, emails, and spreadsheets just to keep things moving. It's the kind of work that quietly eats hours every week.

### With this automation

- Low-stock and reorder alerts before you run out
- Vendor and subcontractor confirmations that chase themselves
- Preventive maintenance scheduled by usage or date
- Work orders and project milestones that update stakeholders
